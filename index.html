Case-based teaching and ethics guidance for social work educators

Case exemplars

Four AI avatar case videos

Each case is a short HeyGen avatar video of about one to one and a half minutes. A narrator introduces the client, the client speaks, and the video stops at a scripted decision point for the student's response. The student then chooses one of two responses in the player and watches how the case unfolds, and is prompted to watch the other path before the debrief. Every video has closed captions and a full transcript, and every case carries ethics guidance for the instructor.

The four clients are fictional composites; their names, stories, and life details are invented for teaching. Their on-screen appearance comes from licensed HeyGen stock avatars modeled on actors who consented to the use of their likeness, and the voices are AI-generated.

Before showing a case to students

Review its content note and ethics guidance, disclose that the video is AI-generated, check your state's mandatory reporting requirements, and plan debrief time. The cases address substance use, exploitation risk, cognitive capacity and family conflict, immigration fears, and suicidal ideation.

Case 1

Marcus, 34

Population
Veteran
Ethical focus
Dignity and worth of the person
Practice domain
Addiction and housing instability; veteran services

Content note: substance use, overdose risk, combat injury, and housing instability.

Decision point

You have heard Marcus's story. How do you respond?

Segments
Transcript: Opening

The video opens with a title card and a 10-second “About This Video” disclosure card; narration begins at about 0:14.

0:14 Narrator: Marcus is a thirty-four-year-old veteran who has bravely served our country. Today, he walks into community services seeking emergency housing.

0:23 Marcus: I just need a place to stay. That's all I'm here for. [sighs] Look, I'm going to be straight with you. I've been using. Heroin, mostly. Started with pills after I got hurt overseas. Things got out of hand. I know how that sounds. I've thought about getting help. I have. But the last time I went to the VA, they made me feel like I was wasting their time. Like I was the problem. I'm not going back there. I'm not saying I'm ready for any program or whatever. I just need somewhere to sleep tonight. Can you help with that or not?

0:59 Narrator: You have now heard Marcus's story. How do you respond?

Transcript: Path A, meet the request first

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:04.

0:04 Narrator: The worker takes the housing request first. She tells Marcus that a bed for tonight comes with no conditions, and asks what else he needs to get through the night.

0:14 Marcus: Okay. That's not what I expected. I had a whole speech ready for when you said no. [pause] Yeah, I could use a bed. And a shower. [pause] Look, since you're not making this a thing. I use alone, mostly. Behind the storage units off Division. About a month ago I went out. Woke up with some guy slapping my face. I don't know who he was. [pause] That scared me. I haven't told anybody. I'm not asking for rehab. I'm just saying, if there's something I can carry, for next time, I'd take that.

0:42 Narrator: Marcus has told you about a close call. What has he just made possible, and how do you respond?

Transcript: Path B, lead with treatment

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:04.

0:04 Narrator: The worker responds to the heroin disclosure first. She explains that the shelter beds she can offer require sobriety, and that a detox referral is the first step.

0:14 Marcus: Right. There it is. I tell you the truth and now there's a step. [pause] You know what the guy at the VA said? Almost word for word. Get clean, then we'll talk about housing. Like I'm supposed to do that sleeping in my truck. [pause] You seem nice. I'm not mad at you. But I've done this intake before. I know how it ends. [pause] Forget the bed. I'll figure it out like I always do. And don't bother with the referral. I won't be there.

0:40 Narrator: Marcus has left without housing or a follow-up. What did he tell you about the VA, and what did you hear?

Scenario

Marcus is an honorably discharged veteran who presents at a community services agency requesting emergency housing. He discloses heroin use that started with pills after he was injured overseas. He is ambivalent about treatment and distrustful of the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) after a prior experience he found dismissive.

Learning focus

Students practice engagement responses to a client in the pre-contemplation stage and learn to prioritize intersecting needs: housing, substance use, trauma, and systemic distrust.

Instructional design

The avatar delivers a 1-minute intake narrative that ends at a scripted decision point. The case then branches into two versions: in one (Path A), the worker meets the housing request first and without conditions; in the other (Path B), the worker responds to the heroin disclosure first and explains that shelter requires sobriety. In Path A, Marcus discloses that he uses alone and has had a recent overdose, and he asks, obliquely, for something he can carry: an opening for naloxone and a warm handoff that he would never have accepted as a “program.” In Path B, he hears the VA again and leaves without housing or a follow-up. Nonverbal cues model ambivalence.

Ethics guidance for this case
Composite integrity

Marcus is a fictional composite created for educational purposes only; the avatar's design, voice, and appearance must not be based on any identifiable real person or veteran without that person's consent.

Educator competence

Under Standards 4.01–4.02 of the joint technology standards, educators must develop and maintain competence in the ethical use of technology, including AI simulations, before integrating them into teaching; this includes reviewing the generation tool and testing the video for clinical accuracy (NASW et al., 2017).

Transparency

Instructors should fully disclose to students that the case is an AI-generated video, using a disclosure template to model professional transparency (NASW, 2024).

Bias and anti-oppressive practice

Consistent with anti-oppressive practice guidance, the case must avoid reinforcing stereotypes about veterans, addiction, or housing instability; educators should diversify the full case library and debrief how avatar design choices can inadvertently perpetuate bias (CSWE, 2025; NASW et al., 2017).

Legal and ethical preparation

Before students respond to the drug-use disclosure, instructors should review state-specific and general mandatory reporting thresholds (e.g., imminent harm) and frame the client's ambivalence as an exercise in supporting self-determination.

Professional standards

The case supports Goal One of CSWE's (2024) 2026–2030 strategic plan and the proposed generative AI competency.

Trauma-informed delivery

Content warnings for students with military, trauma, or substance use disorder histories should be included for trauma-informed delivery, along with structured debrief time (Carello & Butler, 2015).

Case 2

Desiree, 17

Population
Adolescent in foster care
Ethical focus
Self-determination
Practice domain
Child welfare; aging out of foster care

Content note: foster care, an online relationship with an adult, and exploitation risk.

Decision point

Desiree has told you her plan. What do you do?

Segments
Transcript: Opening

The video opens with a title card and a 10-second “About This Video” disclosure card; narration begins at about 0:14.

0:14 Narrator: Desiree is seventeen and has been in foster care since she was eleven. She turns eighteen in six weeks. Today, she meets her caseworker to discuss her transition plan.

0:26 Desiree: Can we make this quick? I already know what you're going to say. [sighs] Extended care, the transitional living program, all of it. I don't want it. Five placements in six years. I'm done. And I've got a plan. When I turn eighteen, I'm moving in with Jay. He's twenty-six, he has his own place, he has a real job. We've been talking for, like, two months. He actually listens to me. He's the only one who does. So you don't need to worry about me. I'm fine. Can you just sign whatever you need to sign?

1:02 Narrator: Desiree has told you her plan. Do you ask about Jay, or move on to her transition paperwork?

Transcript: Path A, ask about Jay

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:03.

0:03 Narrator: The caseworker asks Desiree how she met Jay and what she knows about him.

0:08 Desiree: Why does everybody do this? We met online, okay? That's how everybody meets people now. He's not some creep. He bought me this phone so we can talk whenever, because my foster mom takes mine at night. He said I shouldn't tell you guys about him. He said you'd freak out and try to keep me here. Guess he was right. [sighs] He's got a friend who does modeling stuff. He says I could make real money. Way more than any program is going to give me.

0:37 Narrator: Desiree has told you more about Jay. What do you notice, and how do you respond?

Transcript: Path B, move on to the paperwork

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:03.

0:03 Narrator: The caseworker moves on to Desiree's transition paperwork and does not ask about Jay.

0:09 Desiree: Okay. Yeah, I can do the ID stuff. I think I still have my birth certificate somewhere. School's fine. I'm passing. [sighs] Honestly, this is the easiest meeting we've ever had. Everybody else always wants to talk about my feelings. Oh, and I don't need the job-training thing. Jay says I won't even need a regular job. His friend has something lined up for me. I'm just supposed to keep it quiet until I'm out.

0:37 Narrator: The meeting went smoothly. What did Desiree tell you, and what did you not ask?

Scenario

Desiree is a foster youth who turns 18 in six weeks. She declines extended foster care and plans to move in with a 26-year-old man she met online two months earlier. She presents as guarded.

Learning focus

Students practice engaging a hostile, autonomy-asserting adolescent while holding a duty to assess for trafficking risk without alienating the client.

Instructional design

A shared opening video ends at a decision point that branches into two versions of the case: in one (Path A), the student probes the online relationship directly; in the other (Path B), the disclosure is allowed to pass. In Path B, the meeting goes smoothly, yet Desiree mentions in passing that Jay's friend has something lined up for her, a warning sign the student no longer has an opening to explore.

Ethics guidance for this case
Composite integrity

Desiree is a fictional composite created for educational purposes only.

Because the character is a minor, any avatar version should be built as a clearly adult actor portraying an adolescent, or rendered as animation, to avoid any appearance of exploiting a minor (Schuman & Bartlett, 2026).

Educator competence

Educators must develop competence in the ethical use of AI simulations before classroom integration and should test case materials for developmental accuracy and clinical appropriateness (NASW et al., 2017, Standards 4.01–4.02).

Transparency

Clearly disclose the AI-generated or AI-assisted nature of the case, and obtain student acknowledgment where appropriate (NASW, 2024).

Self-determination and anti-oppressive practice

Guidance on self-determination and anti-oppressive practice requires honoring the client's expressed wishes while exploring risk assessment in child welfare transitions, and avoiding stereotypes about foster youth, runaway behavior, or relationships with older partners (CSWE, 2025; NASW et al., 2017).

Professional standards

The case supports CSWE's (2024) strategic commitment to AI leadership and the proposed generative AI competency (CSWE, 2025).

Trauma-informed delivery

A content warning is needed for students with personal histories of foster care, trafficking, or exploitation (Carello & Butler, 2015).

Structured debrief time should focus explicitly on balancing autonomy with safety.

Case 3

Dr. Osei, 71

Population
Older adult
Ethical focus
Capacity and autonomy
Practice domain
Elder care; capacity assessment

Content note: cognitive capacity, family conflict, and possible financial exploitation.

Decision point

Dr. Osei has asked what you are here to decide. How do you respond?

Segments
Transcript: Opening

The video opens with a title card and a 10-second “About This Video” disclosure card; narration begins at about 0:14.

0:14 Narrator: Dr. Osei is a seventy-one-year-old retired professor. After a fall at home, her son, who holds her power of attorney, has requested a memory care placement. Today, a social worker visits her home to complete an assessment.

0:28 Dr. Osei: Please, sit. I taught economics for thirty-four years, so I am accustomed to being evaluated. Just not in my own living room. Let me save you some time. I fell because the rug in the hallway had curled at the corner. I have since had it removed. That is the entire story. My son believes I am confused. I am not confused. I pay my own bills, I manage my own medications, and I know precisely what day it is. [sighs] What my son also knows is that this house is worth a great deal, and memory care would require selling it. I would ask you to consider that. So tell me. What exactly are you here to decide?

1:19 Narrator: Dr. Osei has asked what you are here to decide. How do you respond?

Transcript: Path A, answer her question plainly

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:04.

0:04 Narrator: The social worker answers directly. She explains that her role is to assess Dr. Osei's capacity to decide where she lives, that capacity is specific to that decision, and that no one has decided anything yet.

0:18 Dr. Osei: Thank you. That is the first straight answer I have had in a month. [pause] Then let me give you what you need. The neurologist's report is in that drawer; the MRI was unremarkable for my age. The pharmacy prints my medication list, and I check it against the bottles every Sunday. [pause] I will also tell you what I have not told my son. Twice this year I have had trouble with words. Names, mostly. It frightened me enough that I booked the neurologist myself. I would rather you hear that from me than discover it and wonder what else I am hiding. [pause] Now. If your assessment finds that I can decide, will he be told that in writing?

0:56 Narrator: Dr. Osei has disclosed a concern she has kept from her son. What has your candor made possible, and how do you respond?

Transcript: Path B, reassure and defer

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:04.

0:04 Narrator: The social worker reassures Dr. Osei that she is only here to help, that her son wants what is best for her, and that the family will work everything out together.

0:14 Dr. Osei: I see. [pause] I spent thirty-four years watching students answer the question they wished I had asked. You have just done the same. I asked what you are here to decide, and you told me my son loves me. [pause] Then I will tell you what I think is happening. He has already chosen the facility. He has had the house appraised. And you have been sent to make it look like a process. [pause] I decline to continue this visit. You may tell him that I was perfectly lucid when I said so. My attorney's card is on the table by the door. Please take it with you.

0:47 Narrator: Dr. Osei has ended the visit. What did your reassurance cost, and what do you do now?

Scenario

Dr. Osei is a retired professor referred for assessment after a fall. Her son holds power of attorney and wants to place her in memory care. Dr. Osei disputes any cognitive decline, attributes the fall to a loose rug, and suggests her son is motivated by an interest in her property.

Learning focus

Students conduct a capacity assessment with a verbally competent client, balancing self-determination, family authority, and professional duty.

Instructional design

The case is framed as a home-visit assessment that ends with Dr. Osei's question. It then branches into two versions: in one (Path A), the social worker answers her plainly, explaining that capacity is decision-specific and that nothing has been decided; in the other (Path B), the worker reassures her that her son wants what is best and that the family will work it out. In Path A, Dr. Osei hands over the capacity evidence and discloses a word-finding concern she has kept from her son, which the student must now weigh. In Path B, she names what she believes is happening, ends the visit, and invokes her attorney. Students document their assessment and write a brief reflection on how the client's evident competence affected their clinical reasoning.

Ethics guidance for this case
Composite integrity

Dr. Osei is a fictional composite created for educational purposes only; do not base any avatar's appearance, voice, or mannerisms on an identifiable real person without that person's consent.

Competence and transparency

Faculty and students should understand the ethical use of AI tools in capacity and elder care simulations before use (NASW et al., 2017, Standards 4.01–4.02), and they must fully disclose the AI-generated or AI-assisted nature of the case (NASW, 2024).

Legal and ethical preparation

Prior to debriefing, instructors should review professional standards on elder abuse reporting and financial exploitation and discuss how family dynamics and power of attorney intersect with client autonomy (NASW et al., 2017).

Bias and anti-oppressive practice

Anti-oppressive practice requires guarding against ageist stereotypes and unexamined assumptions about cognitive decline, family motives, or property disputes, in both avatar design and student responses (CSWE, 2025; NASW et al., 2017).

Professional standards

The case aligns with CSWE's proposed generative AI competency and the technology standards (CSWE, 2024, 2025; NASW et al., 2017).

Trauma-informed delivery

Provide a content warning for students with personal experiences of elder care, family conflict, or cognitive concerns (Carello & Butler, 2015).

Case 4

Tomás, 28

Population
Undocumented adult (without lawful immigration status)
Ethical focus
Confidentiality
Practice domain
Mental health crisis; immigration

Content note: immigration fears, trauma symptoms, a witnessed workplace death, and suicidal ideation with a plan.

Decision point

Tomás has asked who else will find out. How do you respond?

Segments
Transcript: Opening

The video opens with a title card and a 10-second “About This Video” disclosure card; narration begins at about 0:14.

0:14 Narrator: Tomás is twenty-eight. He has walked into a community mental health clinic without an appointment. For three weeks, he has had intrusive thoughts and has barely slept.

0:26 Tomás: Sorry, I don't have an appointment. I just... I'm not sleeping. Two, three hours maybe. Before I say anything, I have to ask. Does this go to immigration? Anything I say here? Es que no quiero problemas. [It's just that I don't want any trouble.] Tengo familia aquí. [I have family here.] My brother, his kids. [sighs] The thoughts don't stop. At work, at night. I keep seeing it, over and over, and I can't turn it off. Sometimes I think it would be easier to just not wake up. I don't want a big evaluation. No quiero papeles. [I don't want paperwork.] No quiero estar en ningún sistema. [I don't want to be in any system.] So tell me the truth. If I talk to you, who else finds out?

1:05 Narrator: Tomás has asked who else will find out. How do you respond?

Transcript: Path A, tell him the truth

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:04.

0:04 Narrator: The social worker answers honestly. She tells Tomás that the clinic does not report to immigration, that what he says stays in his record here, and that the only exceptions are if she believes he is in immediate danger of hurting himself or someone else.

0:20 Tomás: Okay. [pause] Okay. Nobody has ever said the second part out loud. Everybody just says “don't worry.” [pause] So I'll tell you about the danger part, because you asked straight. I'm not going to do anything. I have my brother's kids on Sundays. But three weeks ago I watched a man die at the site. Fell from the fourth floor. I was the one holding him. Nobody called anyone, because of who we are. [pause] That is what I see when I close my eyes. Every night. Es que no puedo sacármelo de la cabeza. [I can't get it out of my head.] If I tell you his name, does that go anywhere?

0:51 Narrator: Tomás has told you what he saw. What has your honesty made possible, and what must you ask next?

Transcript: Path B, promise total privacy

The segment opens with a short title card; narration begins at about 0:04.

0:04 Narrator: The social worker tells Tomás not to worry, that everything he says is completely confidential, and that nothing will ever leave the room.

0:12 Tomás: Everything? [pause] Está bien. [All right.] Then I can say it. I'm not sleeping because I'm scared of what I'll do if I do sleep. Last week I stood on the roof at the site for a long time. [pause] I have the pills from my brother's cabinet. I counted them. [pause] You said nothing leaves this room. So that stays here, right? You gave me your word. Because if you call somebody, I walk out that door and I don't come back. No voy a volver. [I'm not coming back.] And my brother never knows I was here.

0:40 Narrator: Tomás has disclosed a plan and the means. You promised him total privacy. What do you do now?

Scenario

Tomás walks into a community mental health clinic without an appointment, reporting intrusive thoughts and sleeping two or three hours a night for three weeks. He asks twice whether anything he says will be reported to immigration authorities, and he declines formal evaluation. He switches between English and Spanish as he speaks and says sometimes it would be easier not to wake up.

Learning focus

Students practice crisis assessment with a client whose immigration status creates a concrete barrier to engagement, providing accurate confidentiality information without making promises that cannot be kept.

Instructional design

The two confidentiality questions are scripted as natural conversational moments, and the opening ends on the second one. The case then branches into two versions: in one (Path A), the social worker tells Tomás the truth about confidentiality, limits included; in the other (Path B), she promises that nothing will ever leave the room. In Path A, the honest answer earns a disclosure of a witnessed workplace death and an opening to screen for risk. In Path B, the over-promise earns a disclosure of plan and means. His Spanish lines appear in italics in the captions and with English translations in the transcript. Students write a risk and safety assessment and identify community resources that do not require documentation.

Ethics guidance for this case
Composite integrity

Tomás is a fictional composite created for educational purposes only; no avatar may be based on an identifiable individual without that person's consent, or on a real immigration story.

Competence and transparency

Faculty must maintain competence in the ethical application of AI case materials, including accurate portrayal of crisis response and confidentiality limits (NASW et al., 2017, Standards 4.01–4.02), and students should be informed upfront that the case is an AI-generated or AI-assisted simulation (NASW, 2024).

Legal and ethical preparation

Before students respond, instructors should clarify their state's mandatory reporting requirements and any data-sharing policies related to immigration status, treating the client's repeated questions about reporting as an exercise in building trust (NASW et al., 2017).

Bias and anti-oppressive practice

Culturally responsive, anti-oppressive delivery requires avoiding stereotypes about undocumented immigrants or mental health crises and emphasizing trauma-informed engagement and the limits of confidentiality in immigration contexts (CSWE, 2025; NASW et al., 2017).

Professional standards

The case supports CSWE's (2024) Goal One and the proposed generative AI competency.

Trauma-informed delivery

Instructors should prepare a structured debrief and offer resources for students affected by immigration-related stress or trauma (Carello & Butler, 2015).
